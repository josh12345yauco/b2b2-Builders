[ Rowhome Anatomy ]

What a 100-Year-Old Rowhome Is Actually Made Of.

A classic rowhome has two brick party walls shared with the neighbors, and the floor joists span between them — the wooden joist ends sit in pockets built into the brick itself. The interior walls mostly aren’t holding the house up; the brick and the joists are doing the work. That construction is why rowhomes have survived a century — and why their problems are specific:

  1. 01

    Joist ends rot in the wall pockets

    Brick wicks moisture, and where a roof leak or bad repointing let water in, the joist end sitting in that pocket slowly softens. The fix is sistering new lumber alongside, bearing properly — not a jack post and a prayer.

  2. 02

    Old joists are full of notches

    Every generation of plumbers and electricians cut into them. One notch is fine; eighty years of notches in the same joist bay is why the bathroom floor bounces.

  3. 03

    “Non-bearing” walls that aren’t

    Decades of sag can transfer load onto walls that were never meant to carry it. This is why we open ceilings and look before agreeing to remove anything.

  4. 04

    Party-wall work has rules

    Cutting new pockets, adding beams into a shared wall, or underpinning a basement touches your neighbor’s structure too — it’s engineered, permitted work with L&I, and doing it informally is how lawsuits start.

[ Questions ]

Framing Answers.

My floors slope — is my rowhome falling down?

Almost never. A century of settling gives nearly every old Philadelphia rowhome some slope, and most of it is stable. What matters is whether it's still moving and why — a rotted joist end in a party wall pocket, an over-notched beam, or a failing header is fixable, and much cheaper to fix before a renovation than during one. We look, tell you honestly which it is, and price the fix.

Can you remove a wall between my kitchen and living room?

Usually yes. In most rowhomes that wall is carrying the joists above, so removal means a properly sized beam with real bearing points down to the foundation — engineered, permitted, and inspected. It's one of the most transformative changes you can make to a rowhome floor plan, and one of the worst to do casually.

What is joist sistering?

Fastening a new joist alongside a weakened original so they share the load. It's the standard repair for old joists that were notched by plumbers, cracked, or undersized for modern living loads — and while a floor is open during a remodel, it's inexpensive insurance against bouncy floors and cracked tile.

Do you need a permit for structural framing work in Philadelphia?

Yes — structural alterations require an L&I permit, and load-bearing changes typically need an engineer's design. We handle the engineering coordination, the permit, and the inspection. Structural work done without a permit is a resale problem and, worse, an unknown over your family's head.

Do you frame for other contractors or owner-builders?

Yes. Framing is a standalone service for us, not only part of our own builds — we frame additions, full structures, and structural repairs for homeowners, and we work as the framing crew on other builders' projects when schedules align.
