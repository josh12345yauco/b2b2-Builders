[ Built for Winter ]

Why Concrete Fails Here — and What We Do Differently.

Around here, concrete has one real enemy: freeze-thaw. Water gets into the surface or under the slab, freezes, expands about nine percent, and pries the work apart a little more every winter. Everything we do differently traces back to that fact:

  1. 01

    Air-entrained mix on all exterior work

    Microscopic air bubbles give freezing water room to expand without breaking the surface. Skipping it saves a few dollars a yard and costs you the driveway.

  2. 02

    Base depth is non-negotiable

    A slab is only as good as what it sits on. We excavate to proper depth and compact stone in lifts — most heaved Philly sidewalks failed underneath first, not on top.

  3. 03

    Footings below frost depth

    Structural footings go below the local frost line so the ground that freezes can’t lift what we built.

  4. 04

    Control joints, planned — not improvised

    Concrete will crack as it cures; the skill is deciding where. Joint layout is in the proposal, not sawed wherever the crew felt like it.

  5. 05

    No water added at the truck

    Wet mix finishes easier and cures weaker. On hot days the answer is planning the pour, not thinning the concrete.

[ Straight Answers ]

Repair or Replace? We’ll Tell You Straight.

Patching over a failed base is renting a repair, not buying one — and we’ll say so.

Not every cracked sidewalk needs full replacement — a lifted slab with a sound base can sometimes be cut and re-poured alone, and a hairline shrinkage crack in a garage slab is cosmetic, not structural. The estimate names which slabs stay, which go, and why — so you’re comparing real scopes, not just totals.

Slabs vs. footings, in one paragraph: a slab spreads load across the ground — right for floors, sidewalks, and pads. A footing concentrates load and carries structure down below frost depth to soil that can bear it. When someone quotes you a “slab” for an addition that needs footings, that’s not a cheaper option; it’s a different, wrong answer. It’s also why hiring a builder who does both framing and concrete pays off — the structure and what it stands on get designed together.

[ Questions ]

Concrete Answers.

Am I responsible for the sidewalk in front of my Philadelphia house?

Yes — in Philadelphia, sidewalk repair is the property owner's responsibility, and the city can cite you for broken or heaved slabs. We replace sidewalks to city spec, handle the permit and any required inspection, and manage pedestrian protection while the work is open.

Why does new concrete crack, and how do you prevent it?

Concrete shrinks as it cures — cracking somewhere is physics. The professional's job is deciding where: properly spaced control joints give the slab clean places to crack invisibly. The cracks you should never accept come from bad base prep, missing reinforcement, or water added to the mix on a hot day to make finishing easier. We control all three.

How long before I can walk or drive on new concrete?

Foot traffic after 24 to 48 hours, vehicles after 7 days, full design strength at 28 days. We schedule pours so the curing time doesn't collide with your life — and we won't let a driveway get parked on early just because the surface looks hard.

Can you pour a basement slab in an old rowhome?

Yes — it's one of our specialties. Many rowhome basements have thin, cracked, or dirt floors. We excavate as needed, address drainage with stone and a vapor barrier, and pour a proper reinforced slab. If you want more headroom, that conversation involves underpinning the party walls — engineered, permitted work we price honestly because done wrong it endangers two houses.

What is air-entrained concrete and do I need it?

It's concrete mixed with microscopic air bubbles that give freezing water somewhere to expand. For any exterior concrete in Pennsylvania — sidewalks, driveways, steps, pads — yes, you need it. Interior slabs don't. It's a small line on the mix ticket that decides whether your surface scales apart after a few winters of freeze-thaw and salt.
