[ The Honest Version ]

What You’re Actually Paying a GC For.

Any renovation bigger than one room involves five to eight trades, two or three inspection visits, dozens of deliveries, and a hundred sequencing decisions. When homeowners run that themselves, the project doesn’t usually fail — it just takes three times longer, and every delay costs money or a marriage argument. A good general contractor is a professional decision-and-logistics machine. Specifically, we:

  1. 01

    Own the permit path

    We know which scopes need L&I permits, which need licensed-trade sign-offs, and how to file so review doesn’t stall. Unpermitted work is a resale time bomb — we don’t build them.

  2. 02

    Hold the schedule with real authority

    Trades show up for GCs who feed them steady work. That’s leverage a one-time homeowner can’t have, and it’s most of why our timelines hold.

  3. 03

    Catch problems while they’re cheap

    The owner walking the site daily sees the drain line that’s half an inch off before the slab covers it. That inspection instinct is what twenty-plus years in the trades buys you.

  4. 04

    Keep the money legible

    Itemized estimates, capped deposits, milestone payments, and change orders in writing before the work happens — never after.

[ Week By Week ]

What a Week With Us Looks Like.

The person who prices your job is the person who builds it. No sales team, no handoff to a junior project manager you’ve never met.

Every Friday you get the same three things, in writing: what happened this week, what happens next week, and anything that needs a decision from you — with a date it’s needed by. Most renovation delays aren’t caused by slow trades; they’re caused by decisions that arrived late. We surface every decision early and keep a running selections list so nothing ambushes you.

You’ll also always know who’s in your house. We introduce the trades before they start, the site gets locked and broom-cleaned at the end of every day, and if a schedule changes, you hear it from us the day it changes — not from an empty driveway.

[ Pricing ]

How to Read a Bid.

General contracting isn’t usually a separate line you shop for — it’s built into how a project is priced. What you should compare between bids is structure, not just totals:

What to look forA real bid hasA bad bid has
ItemizationLine items by trade and phase, allowances namedOne round number
DepositCapped, tied to materials and mobilizationHalf the job up front
Change ordersPriced and approved in writing before work“We’ll square up at the end”
PermitsNamed in the scope, filed by the contractorNot mentioned, or “you can pull them”

Our pricing promise: written, itemized estimates; a capped deposit with milestone payments; and no surprise change orders.

[ Questions ]

General Contracting Answers.

What does a general contractor actually do?

We take single-point responsibility for your whole project: pricing it honestly, pulling the permits, hiring and scheduling the licensed trades, passing the inspections, holding the schedule and the budget, and standing behind the finished work. You make decisions; we make the phone calls.

Are you licensed and insured in Philadelphia?

Yes. B2B2 Builders is fully licensed and insured for residential and commercial work in Philadelphia and Pennsylvania, and we can provide certificates of insurance — including for buildings that require COIs before work starts.

Do you use subcontractors?

For licensed trades like electrical and plumbing, yes — the law requires licensed specialists, and we work with crews we've known for years. Carpentry, framing, and finish work stay in-house. Either way, everyone on your project answers to us, and we answer to you.

How is B2B2 different from a big design-build firm?

The person who prices your job is the person who builds it. No sales layer, no project-manager handoff, no markup pyramid. The owner is on your site, knows every detail of your project, and picks up the phone. That's not a slogan — it's the whole operating model of a family business.

Do you take on commercial projects?

Yes — small commercial fit-outs and renovations: storefronts, offices, mixed-use buildings with apartments above. The same crew discipline and permit fluency applies; the paperwork is just thicker.
